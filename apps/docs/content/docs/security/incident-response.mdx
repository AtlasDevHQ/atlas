<Callout type="info">
This page is the operational runbook for **Atlas operators**. Customers
looking for the contractual commitment should read
[useatlas.dev/dpa](https://useatlas.dev/dpa) § "Personal Data Breach"; to
report a suspected issue, see
[useatlas.dev/.well-known/security.txt](https://useatlas.dev/.well-known/security.txt)
(RFC 9116) or email `security@useatlas.dev`.
</Callout>

Atlas commits, in the DPA, to notifying an affected Customer **without undue
delay and in any event within 48 hours** of becoming aware of a Personal Data
Breach affecting Customer Data. That is tighter than the 72 hours GDPR Art.
33(1) allows a controller, and it is the deadline this entire runbook is
organized around: every phase below has to fit inside it, and the phases are
ordered so that the notification decision is never the thing waiting on
forensics.

The clock starts at **awareness**, not at detection tooling firing. A page from
an automated probe, a report to `security@useatlas.dev`, and a customer email
all start it equally.

## The 48-hour timeline

| Phase | Target | Gate to leave the phase |
|---|---|---|
| [Detect](#phase-1-detect) | T+0 | Signal acknowledged, incident opened, severity assigned |
| [Scope](#phase-2-scope) | T+0 → T+4h | Affected region(s), data classes, and time window identified |
| [Contain](#phase-3-contain) | T+0 → T+8h | Ongoing access stopped; evidence preserved |
| [Assess notifiability](#phase-4-assess-notifiability) | by T+24h | Decision recorded: is this a Personal Data Breach? |
| [Notify](#phase-5-notify) | by T+48h | Customer notification sent with Art. 33(3) content |
| [Recover](#phase-6-recover) | parallel | Service restored, integrity verified |
| [Post-incident](#phase-7-post-incident) | T+7d | Written review complete |

Containment and recovery run in parallel with the notification track on
purpose. The 48-hour commitment is **not** conditional on the incident being
resolved — Art. 33(3) explicitly contemplates notifying with the information
"then known", and the DPA commits Atlas to ongoing updates as facts develop.
Waiting for a complete picture is the most common way to miss the deadline.

<Callout type="warn" title="DECISION NEEDED — severity tiers">
The phase targets above assume a single "security incident" severity. Real
triage needs tiers, and the thresholds are a maintainer judgement that has not
been made:

- How many tiers, and what are their names?
- What distinguishes them — number of affected workspaces, data classes
  touched (credentials vs. conversation content vs. query results), confirmed
  exfiltration vs. possible exposure, or regulatory exposure?
- Which tiers get the timeline above, and which get a relaxed one?
- Does a **self-hosted** deployment's incident have a tier at all? Atlas has no
  access to a self-hosted customer's data or logs, so the obligation is
  different in kind, not just in degree.

Until this is decided, treat every confirmed incident as if it is the highest
tier. That is the safe failure mode, and it is what the timeline above
encodes.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn" title="DECISION NEEDED — on-call and contact tree">
Atlas is a solo-founder operation. A conventional on-call rotation, escalation
tree, and follow-the-sun handoff do not exist and cannot be invented into
existence by writing them here.

What needs deciding:

- What is the acknowledgement target for an automated page, and does it differ
  between working hours and overnight?
- What happens when the sole operator is unavailable — genuinely unreachable,
  not merely asleep? Is there a delegate, and what access do they hold?
- Which alerting channels are considered reliable enough to carry a page, and
  is any one of them the system of record?
- Is there a retained external party (counsel, a DFIR firm) to bring in above
  some severity, and at what point are they contacted rather than considered?

This gap is load-bearing: the 48-hour clock runs on wall-clock time and does
not pause for availability. An incident that begins on a Friday evening
consumes most of its budget before it is seen.
</Callout>

## Phase 1: Detect

Every mechanism below exists in the system today. Nothing here describes
tooling Atlas does not have.

### The health endpoint's component model

`/api/health` reports a top-level status alongside per-component statuses, and
the distinction between them is the first triage signal.

Top-level status is one of `ok`, `degraded`, or `error`. The endpoint returns
**HTTP 200 for `ok` and `degraded`**, and **503 only for `error`**. The 503
contract is deliberately narrow: only the datasource and, in SaaS mode, the
internal database can promote the top-level status to `error` and pull a region
out of the load balancer. That narrowness is the useful part — a 503 means
platform-critical infrastructure is unreachable, not that some optional
integration is unhappy.

Per-component statuses are `healthy`, `degraded`, `down`, or `disabled`, and
several components can report `down` **without** 503-ing the region. The LLM
provider and the sandbox both behave this way. So do plugins, whose aggregate
status omits `down` from its enum entirely so that one faulty plugin can never
read as a platform outage.

For incident response the reading is:

- **503 / `error`** — platform-critical component unreachable. Availability
  incident; may or may not be a security incident.
- **200 / `degraded`** — something is wrong and the region is still serving.
  This is the status that matters most here, because it is the one a naive
  uptime check treats as success.
- **Component `down` under a 200** — a specific subsystem has failed while the
  region stays live. Worth reading the component out explicitly rather than
  trusting the top-level status.

A deploy-mode downgrade — SaaS requested but enterprise not enabled, so every
SaaS contract silently switched off — surfaces here as at least `degraded`,
beyond the CRITICAL boot log. Treat that as a security-relevant signal, not a
configuration nit: it means contracts you believe are enforced are not.

### Fail-closed boot guards

In SaaS mode a family of boot guards fails the boot layer **before any HTTP
listener starts** when a security-critical contract is violated. They cover, among
others: a missing or malformed encryption keyset, an absent internal database,
an absent per-user rate limit, and a requested-but-unavailable SaaS mode.

Each guard exists because the corresponding misconfiguration previously caused
a silent downgrade — a pod that booted, accepted traffic, and surfaced the
problem at first I/O, first write, or never. The encryption guard is the
sharpest example: without it, every newly stored credential would land in
plaintext through a passthrough path, and nothing would have complained.

Operationally, a guard failure looks like a **process that exits during boot
and never binds a port** — not an error response. In a rolling deploy that
presents as a region that fails to come up while the previous version keeps
serving. The distinction from a crash-loop matters during triage:

- Guard failure — process exits before the listener binds; connections are
  refused rather than answered.
- Runtime fault — listener is up; `/api/health` answers with `error` or a
  `down` component.

<Callout type="warn">
These guards relax to a no-op when the deployment environment is
`development`, so a SaaS-mode process can boot locally without production
secrets. That relaxation is gated solely on the environment value. A
customer-facing deployment running with `development` set has **no fail-closed
guarantees at all**, and confirming that value is one of the first things to
check when a guard "should have" fired and did not.
</Callout>

### The scheduled-backup tripwire

Backup automation can be silently absent while the process boots green — the
enterprise layer failed to load, the gate is misconfigured, the dump tool is
missing, the scratch database is broken. The tripwire answers one question
from the database, independently of the fiber that performs backups: **is the
newest verified backup older than the cadence window?**

If it is, or if no verified backup exists at all, the `backups` component
reports `degraded` — never `down`, because a backup problem must not 503 a
region or pull it from the load balancer.

The cadence semantics matter when reading this during an incident. The
schedule is interpreted as a **cadence window** (length plus anchor offset),
not as a cron tick-matcher: every window gets at most one backup attempt,
claimed atomically so exactly one runs across replicas whenever any replica is
up during the window. This is restart-safe — a window is not skipped merely
because the process was down or deploying at the matching minute. An
unrecognized schedule falls back to a daily default **with a warning rather
than silently disabling backups**, and the fallback is visible to callers.

The practical consequence: `backups: degraded` means recovery capability is
already impaired *before* you need it. Read it as a precondition on
[Phase 6](#phase-6-recover), not as a low-priority warning.

### Administrative audit logging

Administrative operations are written to an append-only admin action log
against a typed catalog of action types in `domain.verb` form
(`workspace.suspend`, `workspace.purge`, `connection.create`, and so on).
Retention is configurable per workspace, and **changes to retention are
themselves logged** — specifically so that a compromised administrator cannot
shrink the retention window to hide their tracks without leaving a record of
having done so.

What this log is good for during an incident: reconstructing what a privileged
account did, and when. What it is **not**: a general-purpose access log. It
captures administrative actions, not ordinary end-user reads. Do not infer
"no data was accessed" from its absence of entries — that is a
different question, and this log does not answer it.

### Externally reported signals

Two inbound paths carry reports Atlas did not detect itself: the address and
disclosure policy published at `/.well-known/security.txt` (RFC 9116, with a
PGP key for encrypted reports), and direct customer contact. Both start the
48-hour clock on receipt if the report is credible. Neither is throttled by
triage capacity, so both need to be checked on a cadence that matches the
acknowledgement target the on-call decision above will set.

## Phase 2: Scope

### Regional blast radius

Under [ADR-0024](https://github.com/AtlasDevHQ/atlas/blob/main/docs/adr/0024-regional-identity-isolation.md),
**residency isolates the entire workspace, identity included**. Each of the
three regions — EU, US, APAC — is a fully independent stack: its own internal
database, its own authentication instance, its own process. There is no shared
identity control plane and no cross-region replication.

Two properties follow, and they are the ones that determine blast radius:

1. **The process *is* the region.** Region is a deploy-time constant, not a
   per-request routing decision. A regional process boots against one
   database, and every row it can touch belongs to that region because no
   other database exists in the process.
2. **A human's identity does not span regions.** The same email address in two
   regions is two independent accounts with separate identifiers and separate
   credentials, related only by the string. There is no global session.

So: **an incident confined to one region's stack is confined to that region's
workspaces.** A compromise of the EU internal database does not expose US or
APAC identities, because those rows are not present in it.

To determine which regions an incident touches, work from the mechanism
outward rather than from the customer list inward:

- **Region-scoped by construction** — anything reached through one region's
  internal database or authentication instance. Scope is that region only.
- **Cross-region by construction** — anything in shared build or delivery
  infrastructure. A compromised container image, dependency, or deploy
  credential reaches every region that consumed it, and region isolation
  provides no protection at all.
- **Determined by the customer's own configuration** — datasource credentials
  point at customer-controlled infrastructure. Blast radius is the customer's
  topology, not Atlas's.

<Callout type="warn">
**One documented exception to regional isolation.** Sandboxed tool execution —
the explore and Python tools — currently runs in the **United States for all
regions**, because the sandbox sub-processor exposes no region primitive. This
is disclosed in the DPA and in the data-residency documentation.

An incident in the sandbox execution layer is therefore **cross-regional by
construction**, even though every other part of an EU workspace is EU-resident.
Do not scope a sandbox incident by region — scope it by which workspaces
executed tools in the affected window.
</Callout>

### Data classes

Scope by what a mechanism can reach, and keep these separate — they carry
different notification consequences:

- **Identity data** — account records, credentials, sessions. Region-scoped.
- **Integration credentials and connection strings** — encrypted at rest in
  the internal database with versioned key rotation. Region-scoped. Exposure
  of ciphertext and exposure of a decryption key are materially different
  findings; establish which one occurred before characterizing the incident.
- **Conversation content and query results** — customer business data,
  region-scoped, and the class most likely to contain personal data the
  Customer is controller for.
- **Customer analytics data** — lives in the customer's own datasource, which
  Atlas connects to read-only. Atlas is not the custodian; a datasource
  incident may still be notifiable, but the containment actions belong to the
  customer.

## Phase 3: Contain

Containment priority is: **stop ongoing access, then preserve evidence, then
restore service.** Restoring service first is the common mistake — a redeploy
can destroy exactly the process state needed to establish scope, and scope is
what the notification content in Phase 5 depends on.

Available levers, described by capability:

- **Credential rotation.** Integration credentials and connection strings are
  encrypted at rest with versioned key rotation, so credentials can be
  re-encrypted under a new key version without re-collecting them from
  customers. Rotating the customer-side secret is a separate action requiring
  customer cooperation, and it is the one that actually revokes access.
- **Workspace suspension.** Suspend, and its reinstate counterpart, are
  logged administrative actions. Suspension is the blunt lever when a specific
  workspace is implicated and the mechanism is not yet understood.
- **Regional isolation as a containment tool.** Because the process is the
  region, taking one region out of service does not degrade the others. This
  is available as a deliberate containment action, not only as a failure mode.
- **Fail-closed by configuration.** The boot guards mean a
  deliberately-invalidated security-critical configuration value results in a
  region that refuses to boot rather than one that boots degraded. This is a
  legitimate emergency stop.

Evidence preservation, before any restorative action: capture the current
`/api/health` response including per-component detail, capture process logs
before they roll, and note the deployed revision. The admin action log is
append-only and retained by configuration, but the retention window is
finite — if the relevant window is near its edge, export before it ages out.

## Phase 4: Assess notifiability

By **T+24h**, a recorded decision: is this a Personal Data Breach affecting
Customer Data?

The threshold is a breach of security leading to accidental or unlawful
destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to
personal data. Note that **destruction and loss qualify** — an incident with no
disclosure at all can still be notifiable. Availability incidents are not
automatically excluded.

Record the decision either way, with reasoning and timestamp. A documented
"assessed, not notifiable, because —" is a defensible position; an
undocumented one is indistinguishable from having never assessed it.

<Callout type="warn" title="DECISION NEEDED — who authorizes notification">
Sending a breach notification to a customer is a legally consequential act
with contractual and regulatory weight. In a solo-founder operation the
operator, the decision-maker, and the signatory are the same person, which
removes the usual separation but does not remove the need for the decision to
be deliberate and recorded.

What needs deciding:

- Who authorizes a customer notification, and is that authority delegable when
  the operator is unavailable? Note that the 48-hour clock does not pause for
  availability, so "nobody can authorize it right now" is a path to breaching
  the commitment.
- Is external counsel consulted before notification above some severity, and
  is that consultation blocking or advisory?
- Who authorizes notification to a **supervisory authority** where Atlas is
  controller rather than processor, and is that a separate decision from the
  customer notification?
- Is there a bias to disclose when the assessment is genuinely uncertain? A
  stated default here is worth more than a case-by-case judgement made under
  time pressure.
</Callout>

## Phase 5: Notify

By **T+48h from awareness**, the affected Customer receives a notification
containing the information GDPR **Art. 33(3)** requires, to the extent known
at the time:

1. **The nature of the personal data breach** — including, where possible, the
   categories and approximate number of data subjects concerned, and the
   categories and approximate number of personal data records concerned.
2. **The name and contact details** of the data protection officer or other
   contact point where more information can be obtained.
3. **The likely consequences** of the personal data breach.
4. **The measures taken or proposed** to address the breach, including, where
   appropriate, measures to mitigate its possible adverse effects.

Send on incomplete information rather than late. "Approximate" and "where
possible" are in the regulation's own text, and the DPA commits Atlas to
providing ongoing updates as facts develop. Each of the four elements should
carry its current confidence — an honest "scope still being established;
current assessment is N workspaces in the EU region" satisfies Art. 33(3)(a)
in a way that silence does not.

Notification goes to the Customer's designated contact. Where the incident is
public-facing or affects availability, the status page and the in-app incident
banner carry the operational narrative in parallel — but they do **not**
substitute for the Art. 33(3) notification, which is a direct, per-Customer
communication.

### Customer-facing communication surfaces

The web application polls the status feed on a five-minute interval and
renders an **incident banner** for any unresolved report. Reports move through
`investigating` → `identified` → `monitoring` → `resolved`, and each report
carries a timestamped update history.

Those four states are the public vocabulary, and they map onto this runbook:

| Status page state | Runbook phase |
|---|---|
| `investigating` | [Detect](#phase-1-detect) / [Scope](#phase-2-scope) — signal confirmed, cause unknown |
| `identified` | [Scope](#phase-2-scope) complete — mechanism understood |
| `monitoring` | [Contain](#phase-3-contain) / [Recover](#phase-6-recover) — fix applied, watching |
| `resolved` | Service restored; [Phase 7](#phase-7-post-incident) begins |

Because the banner clears when the report resolves, and the five-minute poll
means customers see state changes promptly, **status transitions are customer
communications**. Moving a report to `resolved` while notification is still
outstanding sends a contradictory signal. Keep the two tracks consistent.

## Phase 6: Recover

The recovery mechanism for the internal database is **scheduled backups plus
verification**. The tripwire described in [Phase 1](#phase-1-detect) reports on
the newest *verified* backup, not merely the newest attempted one — the
distinction is the whole point, since an unverified backup is a hypothesis
about recoverability rather than a recovery capability.

Before restoring:

1. **Confirm containment.** Restoring into an environment where the access
   path is still open reproduces the incident against fresh data.
2. **Check the tripwire first.** If `backups` was already `degraded` before the
   incident, the newest verified backup is older than the cadence window and
   the recovery point is further back than the schedule suggests.
3. **Establish the recovery point relative to the incident window.** A restore
   that predates the compromise loses legitimate data; one that postdates it
   restores the compromise.
4. **Preserve the pre-restore state** as evidence, per [Phase 3](#phase-3-contain).

Recovery is region-scoped like everything else: restoring one region's
internal database does not touch the others, and does not require taking them
out of service.

## Phase 7: Post-incident

Within **7 days** of resolution, a written review covering: the timeline from
first signal to resolution, how it was detected and whether an existing
mechanism should have caught it earlier, blast radius as finally established,
what was notified and when, and the corrective actions with owners.

The most valuable question is usually the second one. Every detection
mechanism in [Phase 1](#phase-1-detect) exists because something previously
failed silently; an incident that no existing tripwire caught is evidence for
the next one.

<Callout type="warn" title="DECISION NEEDED — incident-record retention">
Incident records — the timeline, the notifiability assessment, notification
copies, and the post-incident review — are themselves a data class, and their
retention has not been decided.

What needs deciding:

- How long are incident records retained, and does that differ for incidents
  assessed as notifiable versus not?
- Where do they live? They must survive the incident, which rules out storing
  them only in the systems they concern. Note that incident records will
  frequently *contain* personal data (affected data subjects, reporter
  identity), so wherever they live inherits residency and retention
  obligations of its own.
- How does incident-record retention interact with the configurable audit-log
  retention window? A post-incident review referencing audit entries that have
  since aged out is a review that cannot be re-verified.
- Are records subject to a legal hold once notification has been sent, and who
  applies it?

Note the interaction with the DPA's audit right: Customers may audit Atlas's
processing once per year. Incident records are the natural evidence for such
an audit, and a retention period shorter than the audit cycle makes that
evidence unavailable.
</Callout>

## What this document deliberately omits

This runbook is public, and a public incident-response document is itself an
attack surface — it tells an attacker what is monitored, how quickly, and what
the response looks like.

Accordingly it describes detection and containment **by capability rather than
by mechanism**: no internal endpoint paths, no credential or key storage
locations, no operator tooling invocations, no alerting thresholds, and no
specifics about which signals are and are not monitored in real time. The
public health endpoint is named because it is already a documented, public
part of the API surface.

Operators needing the concrete commands and paths should work from the
internal deployment documentation. Nothing in this document should be
extended with that detail — if a future edit needs a specific path or command
to make sense, that is a signal the content belongs in the internal runbook
instead.
