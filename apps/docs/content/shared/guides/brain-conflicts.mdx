Two people say different things about the same subject. A stale document contradicts a fresh Slack thread. Sooner or later the brain holds two claims that cannot both be current.

Atlas's answer is to **show you both, with their evidence, and refuse to pick**. This page explains what that looks like, and why it is the design rather than a gap in it.

---

## When a conflict is recorded

Conflicts turn on **predicate cardinality** — whether one subject may hold one value for a predicate, or many:

| Cardinality | Meaning | On a new claim |
|-------------|---------|----------------|
| `single` | The subject has exactly one value (a person has one manager) | The new claim is recorded as being **in tension with** the incumbent |
| `multi` | Values coexist and corroborate (a person knows many languages) | The claims simply coexist |

`multi` is the conservative answer everywhere cardinality is read, deliberately. Coexisting wrongly is recoverable at the review gate; superseding wrongly destroys a belief.

When a `single`-cardinality claim arrives that disagrees with an existing one, Atlas writes an `in-tension-with` edge between them. That edge is a **record of disagreement**, not a verdict.

### Where cardinality comes from

Cardinality is a property of the **canonical predicate**, curated by a human on the **Claim Vocabulary** surface. It is not a field on the claim, and there is **no database default** — absence is the ordinary state and it means `multi`.

A *stored* `multi` is a different fact from absence: it records a human who looked and declined the question, which is what stops the automatic proposer raising it again. Only an **approved** entry counts. A proposer that watches human corrections can suggest `single`; it can never approve one, and nothing autonomous can.

Three readers consult cardinality, and they do not all read the same source — which is what decides whether anything can be destroyed:

| What reads it | Which source | On an uncurated predicate |
|---|---|---|
| The `in-tension-with` edge written **at ingest** | The producer's own per-claim declaration; the extractor's is a model guess | Still flags conflicts |
| The `in-tension-with` edges minted by an **admin tension sweep** | The workspace's currently-approved `single` entries | Mints nothing |
| **Supersession** — closing the incumbent's validity window, at a human publish or correction | The same approved `single` entry | Never supersedes |

<Callout type="warn" title="Contradiction detection works before curation; retiring a belief does not">
  A workspace that has never curated a predicate still sees conflicts. The ingest path flags them from the producer's declaration, and the clusters below are populated normally.

  What curation adds is the **deterministic** half: the sweep that re-examines pairs nothing would otherwise look at again — approving an entry changes what *would* collide for facts already written — and supersession itself.

  The asymmetry is the design, not a gap. An advisory edge is recoverable in both directions, so a model's guess is worth exactly one: a missing edge costs a reviewer a hint, a spurious one costs a glance. Closing a validity window is recoverable by no verb at all, and a curated `single` is **retroactive** — it makes every existing pair in that slot supersedable at the next publish. So Atlas under-flags deterministically rather than retiring beliefs stochastically.
</Callout>

## What a conflict cluster shows

A fact's cluster is its one-hop `in-tension-with` neighbourhood, in **both directions**. Edges are written from the newer claim to the incumbent, so an incumbent that has since been contradicted only ever appears on the receiving side — walking one direction only would hide from you exactly the older claim whose trust is now in question.

Each visible counterpart arrives as a full claim in its own right: subject, predicate, object, review status, validity dates, corroboration count, and **its own provenance**. Not a summary, not a score — the same material you would use to judge the claim it contests.

Counterparts are sorted by fact id. That is deterministic and deliberately meaningless: not by time, not by status, not by source authority, not by corroboration count. Any of those orderings would imply a winner.

<Callout type="info" title="Retired rivals stay listed, and stay labelled">
  A claim that has since been withdrawn or replaced is still why a live claim was contested, so the cluster lists both: it is the one read that does not hide a tombstoned rival, and the one *current-belief* read that does not hide a superseded one. Every counterpart carries the stamps for **both** [temporal axes](/guides/brain-temporal-model#supersession-is-not-retraction) — `invalidatedAt` if it was retracted, `validTo` for the end of its validity window, either or both possible — because neither verb writes `status`. Without them you could not tell a live conflict from one somebody already resolved.

  `validTo` arrives without anyone acting on the rival: the publish gate stamps it on the claim it retires, and nothing deletes the `in-tension-with` edge written when the two claims first disagreed. So a promoted claim keeps its predecessor as a counterpart permanently — correctly labelled as settled, not as an open contradiction.

  Read `validTo` against the clock, not against null. A stamp **in the past** closes the window; a future-dated one is a live rival whose end is merely scheduled, and labelling it settled would hide a conflict that is still open. Both fields are as of now — an `asOf` read does not rewind them.

  Both are **labels, not rankings**. They say what already happened to the rival; the cluster still names no winner.
</Callout>

## Counterparts you cannot see

Conflict clusters cross access boundaries, and this is where the design is least obvious.

Each counterpart is access-checked **independently**, against its own grant — never inherited from whatever entitled you to the fact that owns the cluster. Otherwise being entitled to one claim would hand you a rival claim, and its provenance, purely because the two disagree.

A counterpart you may not see is **reported, not dropped**: you get an opaque handle saying a conflicting claim exists, with no payload. The review surface shows it as a withheld rival; the agent's context window gets an aggregated count.

This matters more than it might seem. "There is a rival you cannot see" is precisely the signal that should stop a reviewer approving a claim or an agent asserting one. An omitted row reads as *nothing contradicts this* — a confident, wrong answer.

## Why Atlas refuses to arbitrate

Nothing in a conflict cluster ranks, scores, or orders by anything implying a winner. That is the point, not a limitation.

An automatic arbitration rule — newest wins, most-corroborated wins, highest-authority-source wins — would have to be right without knowing what the claims mean. Each of those heuristics fails in ordinary cases:

- **Newest wins** loses to a stale correction of a fresh mistake.
- **Most corroborated wins** rewards a rumour repeated in five channels over one authoritative statement.
- **Highest-authority source wins** requires an authority ordering over people that no organisation actually agrees on.

A wrong arbitration is worse than a visible conflict, because it is invisible. The losing claim disappears, and the agent asserts the survivor with full confidence and no signal that anything was ever contested.

So arbitration stays a **human verb**. Resolving a conflict means [superseding](/guides/brain-corrections#supersede--replace-a-belief) the claim that lost — which closes its validity window, records the `supersedes` edge, and keeps the retired belief readable inside its own window — or [retracting](/guides/brain-corrections#retract--the-only-tombstone) a claim that should never have been served.

<Callout type="info" title="What the agent does with a conflict">
  Conflicting facts reach the agent labelled as such, both sides present. The agent surfaces the disagreement rather than silently choosing — an answer built on a contested claim should say so.
</Callout>
